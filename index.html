A carpenter bets on toys

For 66 years, Lego did everything right. The company was founded in 1932 and never posted a loss until 1998.

1932

Toys during the Great Depression

Ole Kirk Christiansen was an expert carpenter when the Depression hit. He figured the one thing people would always find money for was toys for their children.

“Det bedste er ikke for godt.”
Only the best is good enough. The company motto.

When his son Godtfred proudly saved money by giving a wooden duck two coats of varnish instead of three, he got a tongue-lashing for it.

1946

A bet on plastic

Against everyone's advice, the family bought a newfangled plastic injection-moulding machine. Later they adapted the self-locking bricks of Croydon inventor Hilary Fisher Page.

1958

The system

Lego stopped making toys of every shape and size. From now on every brick fitted every other brick, and everything was backwards compatible.

2

Six bricks, endless ideas

915,103,765

A mathematician worked out that just six eight-stud bricks of the same colour can be combined in 915,103,765 ways. That is the power of the system.

6

Half the pieces

Brick colours had grown from the original red, yellow and blue, said to be borrowed from Piet Mondrian, to more than 50. Knudstorp cut the range of individual pieces in half.

13,000different pieces in production
7

Ask the kids

Lego is said to run the largest ethnographic study of children in the world. Its insights team calls it “camping with consumers”: travelling the world to watch how children actually play.

That research produced Ninjago. Test yourself against what six-year-olds told Lego's designers.

90%

of Lego's customers in 2011 were boys. Lego Friends took four years of research and a $40m global marketing push to change that.

Girls could not relate to the blocky Minifigure, so designers made a more detailed Mini-doll. The sets also gained something the boys' sets never had. A toilet. As the article puts it, the girls' pragmatism demanded it.

Quickly build a duck

Lego's story starts with a wooden duck. It ends with one too. At the Lego Foundation, researcher Bo Stjerne Thomsen hands visitors a few red and yellow bricks and asks for a duck. “Everybody can usually do it in 40 seconds,” he says.
